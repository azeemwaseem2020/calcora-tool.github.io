What this calculator can tell you

Use Calcora to estimate future value, total contributions and compound growth from an initial balance plus regular deposits. You can change the rate, time horizon and compounding frequency to compare scenarios.

Compound interest formula

For an initial amount without contributions, A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt). With a contribution at the end of each compounding period, the contribution component is C × ((1 + r/n)^(nt) − 1) ÷ (r/n). If the rate is zero, the result is simply the starting amount plus contributions.

Compound interest with monthly deposits

Selecting 12 compounds per year means the calculator treats each regular contribution as occurring once per month at the end of the compounding period. Real products can credit interest or accept deposits differently, so use the institution's own method when comparing a real account.

Why contribution timing matters

Deposits made at the beginning of a period can receive one additional period of growth compared with deposits made at the end. This calculator uses end-of-period contributions so the assumption stays explicit and reproducible.

How rate, time and compounding frequency affect growth

Longer periods give previous growth more opportunities to compound. Higher assumed rates also increase the projection. More frequent compounding can increase the mathematical result for a nominal rate, but the difference depends on the rate and product terms.

Compare starting money vs regular savings

Two people can reach similar balances using very different paths: one may invest a larger initial amount while another makes regular deposits. Calcora reports total contributions separately so you can see how much of the final value came from your own deposits versus estimated growth.

Example: why assumptions matter

If you enter a 10-year period and an 8% annual rate, the output is a mathematical projection based on that assumption. It should not be read as a prediction that a bank account, stock, fund or other investment will actually return 8% every year.

Inflation, tax and fees

A nominal future balance is not the same as future purchasing power. Inflation can reduce what that balance buys, while taxes and fees can reduce net growth. For a product comparison, add those factors where the product provides reliable figures rather than inventing a universal rate.
